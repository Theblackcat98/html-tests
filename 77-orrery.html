Eisenberger & Holm built about forty of these between 1798 and 1831. Every one has eight arms, because the table of the day called for eight, and on every one the eighth arm is a blank — a socket turned to length and left empty for a planet that had not been found.

It stayed empty for forty-two years. In 1846 a young man in Berlin looked through the right glass and wrote to a newspaper about it, and the arm could finally be filled, but the hole had been in the pattern for so long that nobody who cast the original mould could be found to ask what they had suspected.

On the gearing. The maker is candid on the plaque: true relative motion would put Neptune's arm at one turn in sixty thousand days and Mercury at one turn in eighty-eight. The fitted trains therefore depart from strict ratio by a stated factor, printed beside each wheel. The mechanism is a teaching instrument, not a model, and it says so.

Soli, the fitted lamp

Turn the milled brass collar and the whole mechanism takes one revolution in thirty-one years. There is no gearing between this lamp and the driving weight: the maker wanted the observer to see the arm that does nothing.
