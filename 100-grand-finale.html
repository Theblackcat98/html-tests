    Liquid chrome

    Refraction · specular · surface tension

    Everything begins with a decision about what a thing is made of. The first system in the collection is the most physical: a body of liquid metal that never quite holds its shape.

    Chrome is a cheat, and a good one. It is not a colour — it is an environment, compressed. A chrome surface has no pigment at all. What you see on it is whatever is in the room, bent and stretched and slightly wrong, which is why a chrome sphere under a white studio light reads instantly as a real object rather than a graphic.

    Built here from five orbiting metaballs and one multi-stop radial gradient per body. The gradient is doing the entire job: dark, light, dark, bright, dark, in that order, because that is the order a curved mirror shows you the world in.

    TechniqueCanvas 2D · metaball field
    Shading6-stop chrome ramp per body
    Frame budget2.1 ms

    Wireframe

    Geometry · rotation · the honest skeleton

    Take the surface away and the object admits what it is made of: a list of points, a list of pairs, and a rotation matrix.

    Wireframe is the only visual system in this collection that shows you its own source data. There is no shading, no perspective fudge, no texture. Twelve vertices, thirty edges, one quaternion. It is the drawing a sculptor makes before deciding where the clay goes.

    We use it here as a structural transition. Chrome implies a finished surface; wireframe implies the surface has not been decided yet. Crossing from one to the other is the moment a design stops being an object and starts being a plan.

    GeometryTruncated icosahedron, 60 edges
    ProjectionWeak perspective, 900 mm focal
    RotationTwo axes, constant angular velocity

    Ink wash

    Capillary action · bleed · drying

    Ink is the one medium here that fights back. You do not place it; you release it and then watch where it goes.

    The physics of a wash is a fibre problem. Water follows the paper's grain, carrying pigment with it until the surface tension gives out. The famous blooms — those cauliflower edges that look like continents — are the boundary where the wet front stopped moving and the pigment piled up against it.

    Rendered as thirty multiply-blended radial stains with turbulent edges, drifting at about a millimetre per second. Slower than it looks. Ink never hurries, which is the whole reason people use it.

    MediumPine soot, rice-gel binder
    Paper300 gsm, cold-press, unsized
    Blendingmultiply, 30 passes

    Halftone

    Sampling · frequency · the newsprint bargain

    A halftone is a lie told at a fixed frequency. If you promise the reader there are exactly this many dots per inch, you may keep the tone honest — but nothing else survives.

    Every value in the image becomes a circle. Black is a full cell, white is an empty one, and everything in between is somewhere in between. At arm's length the circles merge into a continuous tone. Lean in and the illusion falls apart into pure geometry, because that is what it always was.

    The screen angle matters more than people expect. Two plates printed at the same angle beat against each other into a visible moiré; rotated fifteen degrees they interlock and read as a third, richer tone nobody printed.

    Screen4.2 px cell, 6.4 px pitch
    AngleDrifting, 15° steps
    Tone8 levels, ordered dither

    Glass

    Refraction · blur · the honest blur

    Glass is the only system in the set that gets its appearance from what is behind it. Remove the background and the glass is nothing at all.

    That dependency is exactly why it is worth rendering. Frosted glass samples its own backdrop across a small kernel and outputs the average — a real, physically-grounded blur that the compositor computes for you, every frame, for free, in a single line of CSS.

    The convincing part is the edge. A pane of glass is not a rounded rectangle; it is a rounded rectangle with a bright rim on the lit side, a dark one opposite, and a slight magnification of whatever passes close behind it. Get the edge right and the middle forgives you.

    SamplesBackdrop, 14 px radius
    Saturation1.4× behind the pane
    Layers4, independent drift

    Pixel art

    Constraint · the grid as a promise

    Pixel art is the only system here that starts by throwing information away and then defends the decision for thirty years.

    Forty-eight by thirty-two. Every mark snaps to that lattice, every colour comes from a palette of six, and there are no anti-aliased edges anywhere. This is not a limitation embraced ironically. It is a claim that the image is composed of discrete decisions, and that you can see each one.

    Scale it up with nearest-neighbour and the decisions become legible. The screen is not hiding behind the image any more. That is the whole argument, and it has been running since 1981.

    Grid48 × 32, integer scaled ×9
    Palette6 colours, hand-picked
    Filteringnone, on purpose

    Gradient mesh

    Interpolation · the soft-edged field

    A mesh gradient has no edges, no bands and no dithering. It is simply a field of colour, and the only question is where you put the poles.

    Six radial gradients, blended additively, drifting on independent Lissajous paths. Where two of them overlap the result brightens; where one fades out the surface is never defined, which is the entire appeal. It is a background that refuses to become a shape.

    Used badly it is wallpaper. Used well — with a very small number of poles, a long period and a lot of empty space — it reads as light in a room rather than as colour on a screen.

    Poles6, screen blend, 40% opacity
    PathsLissajous, 3:2 ratio, 71 s period
    ResolutionIndependent of display

    Riso

    Separation · overprint · happy accident

    Two inks, one sheet, and no registration. Everything that makes risograph printing look like risograph printing is a manufacturing failure that turned out to be beautiful.

    Each plate is laid down separately, in solid fluorescent pigment, through a screen of halftone dots. The paper moves slightly between passes. Where the plates cross, the inks multiply into a third colour that exists in no ink tray — a violet, a near-black, a colour that depends entirely on how crooked the press was that day.

    For this collection the two plates are draggable. The image is not one file. It is two files, an offset, and a rule that says multiply.

    InksFluoro pink · federal blue
    Screen200 lpi, 15° separation
    RegistrationDeliberately ±1.2 mm

    Data

    Density · the honest picture of a system

    The data screen is the least beautiful system here and the most honest. It does not smooth, does not sample, and does not care whether you are ready.

    Four hundred points a second, drawn as they arrive. What you see is not a summary — it is the actual texture of a working machine: gaps, spikes, long quiet stretches where nothing has happened for a while and everything might be about to.

    Any system that claims to compress this into something calm is showing you an opinion, not the data. The comfort of the scrolling log is that it is exactly as ugly as the thing it is reporting.

    Rate380 events / s, unthrottled
    Window12 s rolling, no aggregation
    ColourAmber on slate, plus two alarms

    Blueprint

    Dimension · tolerance · the thing that will be built

    A blueprint is a promise that the drawing and the object can be the same thing. Every line is measured, every measurement is written down, and the whole document is designed to fail loudly if it is wrong.

    This is the last system in the collection because it is the least forgiving. No gradient hides a dimension that does not add up. No texture covers a tolerance that has been ignored. The drawing is only useful if someone can build from it, and the only way to know is to check every number twice.

    Which is the argument the whole collection is making. Ten different systems, ten different vocabularies, one requirement underneath all of them: somebody has to be able to make it, and someone else has to be able to read it.

    SheetA1, 1:5, 0.5 mm line

    All one hundred.

    Ten of them are in this document. The other ninety are not, but they are all here, numbered, in the order they were made. Filter by name, filter by discipline, or hand over the decision entirely.

    100 / 100

    A hundred designs is a hundred decisions about what a screen is for.

    Most of them were wrong at least once. That is the part worth keeping.

    Look at what the ten systems actually share. Chrome needs a room. Wireframe needs a rotation axis. Ink needs paper with grain and nowhere else to go. Halftone needs a promise about frequency. Glass needs something behind it. Pixels need a grid small enough to defend. Mesh needs empty space. Riso needs a misprint. Data needs patience. Blueprint needs a builder.

    None of them is a style. Every one of them is a set of constraints that made a decision easier by making other decisions impossible. That is the argument for working in a system at all: not because it is efficient, but because a constraint you chose is easier to live with than an option you have to earn the right to keep.

    The hundred designs are numbered because numbering is an argument too. It says there is an order, that each one was made once, and that the hundredth is not a hundred times better than the first — only further along. The index is the honest version: a list, a category, a small picture that took longer than the idea behind it.

    Make the thing. Check the dimensions. Let the ink land slightly wrong. Ship it and then read it again in a year, when the fonts have changed and you have not, and see which of the two was the design.
