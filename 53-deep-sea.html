The Descent

Six hours after sunrise the Pacific is a mirror. Take that mirror away and you discover the largest living space on Earth is almost entirely dark. Below two hundred metres the sun gives out, and every photon after that is manufactured by something alive.

60 METRES

The green room

Chlorophyll absorbs red first, then orange, then yellow. What reaches you here is a narrow band of green — which is why the water looks like the inside of a bottle.

240 METRES

The last red photon

A single red photon at 700 nm has already been absorbed within 15 metres. Below this line there is no photography in natural light, no shadows, and no way to tell up from down.

Twilight begins · Mesopelagic · 1% of surface light
700 METRES

Everything here makes its own light

Counter-illumination: fish vent pale bellies that match the dim glow above, erasing their silhouette from below. Some shrimp light their own excrement. The blue-green frequency travels furthest through seawater, so almost every living lamp in the ocean is blue.

~76% of resident animals are bioluminescent · 4 °C
1,400 METRES

Not one animal — many

A siphonophore looks like a single creature and is not. Each bell is a clone specialised for swimming, feeding or reproduction, strung on a shared stem that can exceed forty metres. Some colonies are 800 metres long and are probably the largest organisms that have ever lived.

Praya dubia · colony of 40,000 zooids · 3 °C
2,600 METRES

Midnight, permanently

Four degrees Celsius. Two hundred and sixty atmospheres — a pressure that would collapse an unpressurised submarine instantly. Food arrives only as marine snow: the slow fall of faecal pellets, shed houses and corpses from the sunlit world above.

Bathypelagic · 400 atm · no seasonal variation
4,200 METRES

The lure is a fishing rod

The anglerfish carries a rod tipped with esca, a pocket of luminous bacteria she cannot make herself. She cannot see the blue flare. She feels the pressure change of a body moving through water and opens her mouth.

Abyssopelagic · 420 atm · 2.5 °C
5,400 METRES

Thirty minutes of air

A sperm whale closes its nostrils, rounds its tail under and drops. Its heart slows to two beats per minute. It descends the same vertical column you are scrolling, hunting squid on a single kilometre-wide echo.

Physeter macrocephalus · 45-minute recorded dive
6,000 METRES

Floor of the world

The Challenger Deep. 1.1 gigapascals. Sediment older than the Atlantic. The pressure at the bottom of this column is roughly 600 times what you feel at sea level, and it has never been higher.
