Paper-cut study  ·  No. 03  ·  Nine sheets of 300 gsm

NineSheets

A sunrise made the slow way: nine sheets of dyed cardstock, one blade, and about forty minutes of cutting that cannot be hurried. Each layer sits a few millimetres above the last, so the low morning light rakes across the cut edges and every ridge throws its own small shadow onto the hill behind it. That stack of shadows is the whole trick.

The cut list, in the order the sheets were laid down

Every silhouette below is a single polygon — no drawing, no brushes, no printing. The paper was dyed in a tray, dried flat under weight, and cut against a green mat with a fresh blade. Rough edges were left on purpose: a clean machine cut looks like plastic, and plastic has no weight.

Adhesive

Sections only. A thin PVA run on the back of each sheet, pressed under a book for twenty minutes. Glue stick only on the two cloud slips — they are the first thing to yellow, and they sit far enough forward that it does not matter.

Light logic

One low sun, off the right shoulder of the valley, so every cut edge throws its shadow down and to the left. Flip the light and the whole diorama re-reads: the hills start to climb out of the frame instead of settling into it.

Colour

Five baths, one recipe: madder root for the reds, ochre for the sky, iron for the darks, all cut with a trace of indigo so the shadows stay warm instead of going grey. Nothing here is mixed on the palette — the tint comes from the bath.

Care

Keep it out of sunlight and away from radiators. Brush the dust off with a dry pastry brush along the grain, never across it. The torn foreground edge is meant to fray a little more with age. That is not damage; that is the paper remembering.
